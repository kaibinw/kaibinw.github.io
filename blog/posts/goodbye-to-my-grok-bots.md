# Goodbye to my Grok Bots

_For months, eighteen bots woke up before me, read the internet while the house was still quiet, and left a neat stack of notes by my coffee._

They never complained about the hour. While the rest of the street was still asleep, they combed through news feeds, release notes, and research threads, then lined up their findings so I could read them over breakfast. If you read the first ten posts on this site, you have already met them—they were the original cast behind my day-one plan and the org charts that followed.

If you are just starting out with AI, there is something genuinely magical about that first morning when an automated helper hands you work it finished while you were dreaming. I will miss that feeling.

## The quiet parting

The goodbye itself was simple. A39 a month—roughly A460 a year—due to renew on 20 November. I let the subscription lapse, and with it went the morning crew: the helpers that wrote my daily briefings, watched for software updates, and pulled together the week in review.

At the end, eighteen of them were still on the roster. Ten were showing up every morning. Eight had been sitting frozen since a usage limit paused them on 23 October, and the hardest truth to admit was how little I noticed their absence. When half a team goes quiet and your week runs just the same, the season has already ended.

## How hard it was to keep them fed

I want to be honest about why it ended, because from the outside, running eighteen bots looks effortless. It wasn't.

At the start, I was genuinely struggling just to keep them pointed in the right direction. Every single bot needed its own job, its own instructions, and enough background to know what mattered. Even after I wired them into Notion so they had a place to read from and write to, keeping eighteen separate helpers aligned was heavy work.

**They lived too far from my real desk.** My bots ran in the cloud, while my notes, plans, and settled decisions sit on my computer at home. Even with Notion bridging the gap, I still had to carry context over by hand so they wouldn't spend their morning researching questions I had already answered three weeks earlier.

**I still had to check every line.** Nothing a bot wrote could go straight into my permanent notes or guide a real household decision without me reading it first. That meant every morning began with me grading homework I had paid to have written. You can be fond of the crew and still admit the routine has grown heavier than the help.

|What the bots did|What it took to run them|What they gave me|
|---|---|---|
|Morning research briefings|A$39 a month, plus my checking time|A thoughtful draft waiting with my coffee|
|News and update sweeps|Separate instructions and Notion wiring|Good leads, once I double-checked them|
|Weekly wrap-ups|Constant tending to keep them on track|A neat summary of the week|

## What stays behind

Letting them go wasn't a failure—it was graduation. Those first ten posts exist because eighteen bots forced me to figure out how to divide work, write clear instructions, and check what an AI hands back. Every habit I use now started with trying to keep that noisy, eager morning crew from tripping over each other.

If you are building your own setup and wondering when it is time to retire a tool you have grown attached to, ask yourself one honest question:

**Am I spending more energy feeding this helper than it saves me?**

When the answer turns into a quiet yes, it is okay to thank them and close the tab.

So, goodbye to my eighteen morning bots—the original cast of this blog. You were early, you were tireless, and you made those quiet mornings feel a little less solitary. Thank you for getting me started.
